# Prior Research (seed)

> **Status:** Carried over from an earlier Claude chat, before this project started. Sources weren't kept, and the percentages are directional (mostly blogs plus vendor research such as Figma's). Treat this as a starting hypothesis for the Phase 1 market research in `market.md`, not as findings. The redo happens with Molly in S1, tailored to her target roles and companies, and cites its sources.

## What the research says (and why it shapes the outline)

- **Skim time is short.** One source puts the homepage at 10–15 seconds and the full review at 2–3 minutes. That's why the summary has to work without scrolling.
- **Judgment beats polish.** AI makes finished screens cheap, so mockups alone read as generic. Hiring managers say they want real decisions, including wrong turns, and what changed because of the work.
- **Fewer, deeper, and domain-specific.** The consensus is 3–5 focused case studies, not a gallery. Ed tech depth is an advantage for her.
- **AI fluency is expected.** 73% of hiring managers in Figma's survey see growing demand for it. Case studies should show what the AI did, what she decided, and one moment it was wrong.
- **Craft still counts, framed as judgment.** 58% rank visual polish in their top five skills, while NN/g argues UI is becoming less of a differentiator. Her typography and accessibility work should be presented as reasoning, not decoration.
- **AI tools parse portfolios too.** Her name and role should be in the page title and meta description, with clean headings.
- **Generic reads as generated.** Hiring managers have gotten good at spotting templated, AI-written portfolio content.
- **Seniors are in demand.** 56% of hiring managers report rising demand for senior designers versus 25% for junior ones.

## Draft case study outline

1. **Summary card, above the fold:** a one-line hook, her role and scope, timeline and domain, two or three headline outcomes, and one takeaway sentence. Optionally a 3–5 minute walkthrough video.
2. **The situation:** the stakes and constraints, kept short.
3. **The hardest call:** the longest section. Options considered, what she chose and rejected, where she was wrong or changed course.
4. **The craft:** two or three specific typography or accessibility decisions, with reasoning and annotated real screens.
5. **How it got built:** engineering collaboration and what shipped differently than designed.
6. **Where AI fit:** what the agent did, what she decided, and one catch where she corrected it.
7. **What happened:** outcomes, then what she'd do differently.

## What keeps it from reading as generated

- Her own phrasing and opinions, not smoothed corporate language.
- Details only she would know, like a stakeholder objection or a late accessibility bug.
- Uneven section lengths, with no stock "Challenge / Solution / Results" triplets.
- Real artifacts such as sketches, specs, and audit results.

## Portfolio-level pieces that support it

- A one-page impact summary per case study for people who won't click through.
- Older pre-AI work condensed into a short "selected earlier work" section.
- A clean, two-page, ATS-safe resume with education on page two.
- Referrals alongside applications, since referrals are roughly 7% of applicants but an estimated 30–50% of hires.

## Confidence (as originally stated)

No proven "correct" case study structure exists. The outline combines the hiring themes with common practice, so test it by having a non-designer skim the summary card.
