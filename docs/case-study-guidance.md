# Case Study Guidance

What Phase 1 settled about how Molly's case studies work. Phase 3 (writing) and Phase 4 (templates) build from this.

*Locked 2026-10-02, from the Phase 1 research and Molly's reactions to it (Pocket recording "Design Portfolio and Case Study Strategy").*

## Principles

1. **Two layers.** Each case study opens with a summary that works on its own for a reader who never scrolls. The depth sits below it. The page should make a reviewer *want* the deep dive, which then happens in the interview.
2. **No word ceiling.** The research trend toward 500–800 words is rejected. Authentic voice matters more than brevity, and her projects can't be explained in 500 words. Be concise, but go deep enough to show she understood the problem and how she solved it.
3. **3–5 case studies, three minimum.** With real variety, reviewers pick the ones relevant to them, so cards and the portfolio page need clear signals of what each case study is about. The site isn't tailored per application; interview presentations are.
4. **Outcome first.** Lead with the finished product and a quick problem/solution summary, then the process for those who want it. No Double Diamond or "Empathize → Define → Ideate" structure.
5. **Decisions and trade-offs.** Show the messy reality: compromises, what didn't make the MVP and how it was set up for later, where plans changed. This is what separates real product work from a school project.
6. **Metrics are available.** Every project had tracked goals. Use them.
7. **AI both ways.** Make explicit that she designs *with* AI and designs AI *products*. "Good with AI, and good on AI."
8. **Craft supports, doesn't compete.** Don't over-design the site; flashy UI is cheap now.
9. **Show scope, not titles.** Emphasize platform-wide adoption. Use language that shows she operates at a staff level without claiming the title. Show autonomy and the many hats she wears through specifics, not as a buzzword.
10. **Specialist: K-12 ed tech.** Lead with it firmly.
11. **Her voice.** Molly writes the copy. Claude helps her brainstorm it; it doesn't write it for her.
12. **Show the work, in the page.** Embed working prototypes directly in the case study rather than linking out or using screenshots, one per case study where one exists. Prototypes over video, since video takes much more time and effort to produce.

## Section Suggestions

Not a template. Each case study is shaped by its project, and Molly picks what fits.

**Every case study has:**
- **The summary (top):** the finished product, a quick problem and solution, her role and scope, timeline, domain, and headline outcomes
- **A retrospective (end):** she always does one, and it lands well with reviewers

**And may include some of the following:**
- **Context and stakes:** who it's for (teachers, students, administrators, districts) and why it mattered to the organization
- **Constraints:** time, technology, privacy, accessibility, district and buyer realities
- **The real problem:** what the problem turned out to be, if it differed from the brief
- **Discovery:** interviews she led and what they surfaced
- **The hard call:** options considered, what she chose and rejected, and why
- **Trade-offs:** what didn't ship and how it was structured to come later with minimal disruption
- **Where it changed course:** a wrong turn, and what changed her mind
- **Designing with AI:** how AI shaped her process, and where her judgment overrode it
- **Designing the AI product:** model behavior, teacher trust, guardrails, keeping educators in control
- **Craft details:** typography, accessibility and interaction decisions, with the reasoning
- **User testing:** what she did, the results, what she learned and changed (only when testing happened)
- **Building it:** working with engineering and product, what shipped differently than designed
- **Rollout and adoption:** how it reached teachers at platform scale
- **Outcomes:** results against the project's goals
- **The prototype:** embedded and playable
- **Annotated screens:** sticky-note-style notes that narrate her thinking
- **Evolution:** for multi-year programs, how the pieces built on each other
- **What's next:** for ongoing work

## Project Shapes

The story arc depends on the project's shape more than its topic:

| Shape | Example | How the story differs |
|---|---|---|
| Multi-year program | Teaching Assistant: several smaller projects over ~3 years culminating in one product | Told across stages; the Evolution section carries it; outcomes can be shown per stage |
| End-to-end project | A 3–6 month project, start to ship | The classic arc, with one central hard call |
| Shipped, then ongoing | Work that launched and kept receiving investment or maintenance | Ends with what happened after launch and what's next |
